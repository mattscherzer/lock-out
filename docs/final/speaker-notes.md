# LockOut final talk: speaker notes

The talk runs about 10 minutes: roughly 7 minutes of speaking and a 2.5-minute live demo. At about 140 words a minute the script below is about 900 spoken words.
Each slide ends on a line that hands over to the next, so nobody starts cold. Text in brackets is a stage direction, not something to say.

## 01 · LockOut (Burcak, then Fritz)

[The slide is already up while people settle. The map fills in.]

**Burcak:** Hi everyone. I'm Burcak.

**Fritz:** And I'm Fritz.

**Burcak:** And before we tell you what LockOut is, think about the last time you locked your bike somewhere and just... hoped. Keep that feeling in mind, because that's where this starts.

## 02 · You lock it. It's gone. (Burcak)

[Advance. The three lines appear one by one, then the lock drops from the post.]

You lock it. You come back. It's gone. This is Lena. She lives in Neukölln, she locked her bike outside her flat at half past ten, and at quarter to eight the next morning there was just an empty lamp post. It was her second bike in two years. Lena is a composite of the riders we designed for, but if you live in Berlin, you probably know a real Lena. Maybe you are one. And she's not alone.

## 03 · Every 23 minutes (Burcak)

[Advance. The clock hand sweeps round.]

Every twenty-three minutes, a bike is stolen somewhere in Berlin. Last year the police recorded twenty-two thousand six hundred thefts. That's sixty-two bikes a day, and on average each one cost its owner about twelve hundred and fifty euros. And these are only the cases people reported. But here's the part that surprised us most.

## 04 · 94 of 100 were locked (Burcak)

[Advance. The grid of locks pops in, a few gaps among them.]

Ninety-four out of every hundred stolen bikes were locked. The police record whether a lock had to be broken, and almost every time it did. So people aren't careless. The lock was there. It just wasn't the right lock, for the right place. Which makes you ask: why don't people know what the right lock is?

## 05 · The gap (Burcak, hands to Fritz)

[Advance. Dots spread over the map on the left, then the quote appears.]

Partly because of what's out there today. Crime maps look like this: dots everywhere. Busy areas look terrifying, quiet ones look fine, and nothing tells you what to actually do. But when we talked to riders, the question was always the same: "I'm parking here, tonight. Which lock?" And the usual answer, "just buy a good lock", doesn't answer that at all. Fritz, so what did we build?

## 06 · Police data in, one clear answer out (Fritz)

[Advance. The three steps slide in left to right.]

Thanks, Burcak. The idea fits in one line: police data goes in, one clear answer comes out. It's three steps. First, you find your spot: type a street, or let the app find you. Second, you see how it compares: low, medium or high, measured against the whole of Berlin. And third, you lock it right, with advice that comes from the police and cycling experts, not from us. Let me show you what that looks like across the city.

## 07 · 542 neighbourhoods (Fritz)

[Advance. The map colours in, the bar grows, then the Alt-Treptow label appears.]

We took a year of police reports and rated every one of Berlin's five hundred and forty-two neighbourhoods. Most of the city is low. A hundred and thirty-five are medium, and eighty-two are high. Look at Alt-Treptow up here: two hundred and fifty-two reports in a single year. And the hatched areas are the ones where there just aren't enough reports to say, but I'll come back to those. First, Burcak, what does the colour actually tell you to do?

## 08 · A calm street is never a reason to lock less (Burcak)

[Advance. Headline first, then the two columns.]

The most important rule first: a calm street is never a reason to lock less. The colour tells you how careful to be, but there's a floor. If your bike is worth under a thousand euros, use one solid lock, and lock the frame and a wheel to something fixed. If it's worth a thousand or more, use two locks of two different types, because thieves usually specialise in one kind of tool. Something you can follow in ten seconds at the bike rack. But that's enough slides. Fritz?

## 09 · Let's try it (Fritz drives, Burcak plays the rider)

[Advance. Leave the slide up for about five seconds so people can read the three steps, then switch to the app.]

**Fritz:** Let's try it, live. Three steps: find where I am, read the answer, pick the lock. Burcak, you're the rider. Where do you lock your bike?

**Burcak:** [Name the street you rehearsed.] Usually right outside the café there.

**Fritz:** [Type the street and select the area.] So here we are. A colour, and underneath it, what it means in plain words, compared to the rest of Berlin.

**Burcak:** [React honestly as a rider, for example:] I would not have guessed that. So what should I do?

**Fritz:** [Scroll to the lock advice.] That's the bit I like most. It doesn't just say "risky", it tells you what to bring. [Read out the recommendation.] [If there's time, tap "Use my location" to show it finding the area you're standing in.]

[Switch back to the deck.]

Backup if the network or the app fails: stay on this slide, point at the phone picture and say the three steps out loud with Wrangelkiez as the example: high, among the fifteen percent of areas with the most thefts, use two locks. Then move on. Don't try to fix it live.

## 10 · When we don't know, we say so (Fritz)

[Advance. Three boxes: many dots, some dots, hatched.]

Remember the hatched areas? This is one of the choices we're proudest of. Where there's plenty of data, you get a rating you can lean on. Where there's some, it's our best read, and it could shift. But where there are only a couple of reports, we don't give a rating at all, and we never call it "safe". Two thefts in a quiet street doesn't mean it's safe; it means we don't know yet. And that honesty runs through the whole app: no accounts, no tracking, and the code is open for anyone to check. So where do we take it from here? Burcak?

## 11 · Berlin is only the first city (Burcak)

[Advance. The dotted line keeps moving to the right.]

Today, LockOut is live for Berlin, all five hundred and forty-two neighbourhoods, on any phone. Next, we want to test it on real streets with real cyclists, because so far we've designed for riders like Lena on paper. And after that, any city where the police share their theft data could have the same thing. Berlin is only the first city.

## 12 · Danke (both)

[Advance. Stay on this slide through the questions so people can scan the code.]

**Burcak:** So if there's one thing to take home: try it tonight, before you lock up. Just scan the code.

**Fritz:** And if you've got your own bike story, or an idea for us, find us on LinkedIn. We'd really love to hear it. Danke, thank you for listening.
