# User Research: Individual Work

Individual work, user research steps 1–4, 22 October 2026.

Three AI personas (Lena, Murat and Jonas from `docs/discovery/step-1-personas.md`) reacted to the demo prototype. Each persona ran in a separate chat with the same setup message, screenshots and questions.

## Setup

Screenshots shown (Evening, Weekday, Standard bike):

- Storkower Bogen (Lichtenberg): High risk, Confidence High
- Charitéviertel (Mitte): Low risk, Confidence Medium
- Velodrom (Pankow): Medium risk, Confidence High

All ratings and numbers in the prototype are mock data.

## Questions

1. You've just opened this on your phone. In your own words, what is this app telling you, and what's the first thing you would do with it?
2. The app rates each area as Low, Medium or High risk, with a confidence of Low, Medium or High. Looking at the three cards, what do these ratings mean to you, and how much would you believe them?
3. Think of the last time you locked your bike outside. If you had checked this app beforehand, what, if anything, would you have done differently?
4. How do you decide today where to park and which lock to use? How does this app compare to that?
5. What would stop you from using this app regularly, or make you stop after trying it once?

## Lena

### Q1

Okay, so it’s a map of Berlin chopped into lots of little coloured patches, and it tells me how likely my bike is to get nicked in each one, plus which lock I should use there. Honestly, my first reaction is that the whole city looks pink and yellow, which doesn’t exactly calm me down. The first thing I’d do is type in my own street in Neukölln to see what colour my lamp post is, because that’s the only thing I really care about. Then maybe my office, since the bike sits there all day. I’d probably skip the Morning/Evening and Weekday buttons at first; I’m not even sure I’d notice they’re already set to something.

### Q2

Low, Medium and High I get straight away, and I actually like the sentence under it, "more than in most of Berlin", because comparing my street to the rest of the city is exactly what I'm missing. The confidence part confuses me, though. "High risk" and "Confidence: High" right under each other just reads like "really, really high" until I stop and think about it. The word "reported" also makes me doubt it, because I never reported my stolen front wheel, and after the whole bike went, the police portal felt like it just swallowed my report. So I'd believe it as a rough vibe, but not as the truth about my exact corner.

### Q3

Last night around half ten I locked it to the lamp post outside my flat, same as always: U-lock round the frame and the post, cable through the front wheel. Honestly, even if the app had said High, I wouldn't have done anything differently that night, because there's nowhere else for the bike to go. The one thing that would have stuck is "lock the frame and rear wheel", because my U-lock usually just goes round the frame. I'd also have wanted to check if my lock is one of these "VdS A+/B+" ones, but I have no idea what that means or where it would even say it on the lock. So maybe it changes how I lock, but not where.

### Q4

Honestly, I don't really decide: I pick a pole where it looks busy and well lit, and I lock the same way every time without thinking about it. The app turns it into a decision every time, with a time of day, a bike type and different lock advice per area, and I'm not going to carry two locks to the supermarket and one to the pharmacy. What it could do that my gut can't is tell me whether my street is actually worse than the one round the corner. But it also messed with my own rule: Storkower Bogen lists "shops and cafés keep eyes on the street" and it's still High risk, so now I don't know if busy is good or bad. I'd look at it once to sort out my routine, not before every stop.

### Q5

Mostly that once I've looked up my street, my office and maybe where my friends live, there's nothing new to come back for, because my places don't change. And if my street comes up pink, the app just turns my background worry into a pink label without giving me anywhere else to put the bike, which is the opposite of peace of mind. It also never tells me whether the lock I already own is good enough, which is the one thing I actually want answered, because "VdS class B+ or similar" means nothing to me. If it just said "your lock is fine" or "get this one, around €90", I'd have something to show my flatmate and I'd be done. As it is, I'd try it once, feel slightly worse, and forget about it.

## Murat

### Q1

Okay, so it's a coloured map of Berlin, pink is bad, green is good, and it tells me which lock to use for an area. The first thing I'd do is type "Bahnhof Spandau" in the search box, because that's the only place I really care about. These names like "Storkower Bogen" or "Charitéviertel" don't mean much to me, I think in stations and streets, not in areas. And honestly, why is it in English? If a colleague sent me this link, I'd expect German, otherwise I'd probably close it after a minute.

### Q2

High risk means lock everything, low risk means you can relax a bit, that much I get. But then there's a second "High" and "Medium" for confidence, with these little dots, and I had to read it twice to understand it's not about the bikes. I'm not sure I'd believe it much, because when my saddle and my wheel got stolen, I never reported it to the police, so who is counting that? "Low risk" for Charitéviertel might just mean people there don't bother to report. I'd trust it more if it said where the numbers come from in one line, and I'd still ask my colleagues first.

### Q3

This morning at ten to six, Spandau station, same rack as always, one U-lock through the frame and the back wheel. Honestly, I don't think I'd do anything differently, because I don't have a choice where to park, the station is the station. The buttons only let me pick Morning or Evening, but my bike stands there from six to four, so which one am I supposed to press? If it said "high risk", maybe I'd carry a second lock for a week, but it doesn't say anything about my saddle, and that's what they took from me.

### Q4

Where I park is simple: at the station, because there is nowhere else, and I copy Heiko from technical services, who leaves his at the far end near the kiosk and has never lost anything. Wait, I shouldn't invent a name, let me say it plainly: I copy the colleague who has never lost a bike. For the lock, I go to the shop, pick it up, check the price and how heavy it feels in my bag. The app talks about "VdS class A+/B+", I have no idea what that is, and it doesn't tell me what it costs or how much it weighs. And it tells me the whole area is bad, but not which corner of the station is safer, and that's what my colleague can tell me in five seconds.

### Q5

The biggest thing: I park at the same station every single day, so once I've seen "Spandau, high risk", why would I open it again tomorrow? It's the same answer, and it doesn't change anything I can do. If it's in English and I have to zoom around a colourful map on my phone at ten to six in the morning, I'm out after one try. And if nobody at work is using it, I'd forget it exists by next week. Maybe the only thing that would bring me back is if I could show my wife a clear number for my station, but this doesn't give me that in a way I could just send her.

## Jonas

### Q1

It's a theft-risk map of Berlin: pick a time of day, weekday or weekend, and bike type, and it rates the area low, medium or high and tells me which lock to use. Honestly, my first impression is a very busy patchwork of pink, yellow and teal, and on a phone screen I'd struggle to make sense of it at a glance. The first thing I'd do is type in my own Kiez in Prenzlauer Berg, then wherever I'm heading tonight, to see whether it matches what my gut already tells me. Then I'd go looking for where to tell it I'm riding a €2,800 gravel bike. "Standard bike" vs "E-bike / cargo" doesn't really describe my situation.

### Q2

I read the rating as "compared to the rest of Berlin", which helps, but low in Berlin still isn't low. Showing the confidence separately is the part I like most; it feels honest, and I'd trust the label more because of it. What bugs me are the reasons underneath: "Fewer people around after dark" appears on all three cards, so it feels templated. "Shops and cafés keep eyes on the street" sits under High risk, which sounds like a reason for less risk, not more. And "146 reported thefts in the data window" doesn't tell me over what period or in how big an area, so I'd treat the colour as a rough hint, not something I'd bet my bike on.

### Q3

Last Saturday I left the gravel bike outside a café in Kreuzberg for about two hours after a ride, with just the lighter lock because I didn't want to haul the 1.5 kg U-lock. If the app had said high risk, I might have taken the heavy lock that morning, and that's the only decision it could realistically have changed. Where I lock it, I'd still have chosen the way I always do: lighting, other bikes, people around. A whole neighbourhood colour can't tell me which side of the street is better. And it says nothing about two hours versus twenty minutes, which matters more to me than the district.

### Q4

I decide in two steps: in the morning I pick which bike and which lock to take based on where I'm going and for how long, and at the spot I judge it by eye, with good light, other bikes and people walking past. The app only fits that first step, and I do like that it speaks my language with VdS classes instead of "use a good lock." But it gives the same advice whether I'm leaving the gravel bike or the €300 errand bike, and that's exactly the decision I need help with. On the street, my own eyes beat a coloured district every time. The anchoring tip is fine but basic; I've known that for years.

### Q5

If checking takes more than a few seconds, I won't do it before going out. Right now I'd have to type an area name and read a whole panel. The bigger risk is that I learn my usual spots after a week, the colours never change, and there's no reason to open it again. If the reasons keep contradicting themselves, or it ignores what my bike is worth, I'd decide it's another scary crime map and delete it. What would keep me is an answer to my real question, "is my lock enough for this bike, here, for two hours?", ideally something I could screenshot for my insurance or send to friends.

## Real User Interview Guide

Builds on `docs/discovery/interview-guide.md` (recruiting, screening and the 30-minute structure stay as they are). This section adds what to test after the synthetic interviews.

### Hypotheses to test

| # | Hypothesis | Comes from | Wrong if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| H1 | Riders check the app once for their usual places and don't come back | Pattern: no reason to open again (all three); team assumption A2 | Riders describe regular moments when they'd check it (new places, trips, nights out) |
| H2 | Riders rarely choose where to park, so the lock advice matters more than the map | Patterns: area colour doesn't help choose; app changes how, not where; team assumption A3 | Riders regularly choose between several spots and would use the map for it |
| H3 | Riders look for places by street, station or Kiez, not by planning-area name | Pattern: search only finds area names (all three) | Riders find their spot easily by tapping the map or by area name |
| H4 | Riders who never reported a theft distrust "reported thefts" figures | Pattern: doubt about "reported" (Lena, Murat); team assumption A7 | Riders trust the ratings even after hearing that many thefts go unreported |
| H5 | Riders read the confidence label as part of the risk, not as how sure the app is | Pattern: confidence label confuses (Lena, Murat) | Riders explain "High risk, Confidence: Low" correctly in their own words |
| H6 | The time-of-day filters don't fit how long riders actually park | Pattern: time filters don't fit (Murat, Jonas) | Riders pick a time filter without hesitation for their usual parking |
| H7 | Riders don't know their lock's class and need advice in terms they recognise (type, price, weight) | Pattern: lock advice uses unclear terms (Lena, Murat) | Riders know their lock's rating or understand "VdS class B+" |

### Interview questions (steps 2–4 of the team guide)

Ask these before showing the prototype.

- **H1 Coming back:** "Think of last week. Before which trips, if any, did you think about where or how to lock your bike?"
- **H2 Choosing where:** "Think of the places you left your bike last week. At which of them could you have chosen a different spot?"
- **H4 Reporting:** "Has a bike or part ever been stolen? What happened next?" (team guide) / "Did you report it to the police? Why or why not?"
- **H6 Parking time:** "The last time you left your bike outside, how long did it stand there?"
- **H7 Lock:** "Which lock do you own and why?" / "How do you know whether your lock is good?" (team guide)

### Prototype tasks (replace the single mock screen in step 5)

Plan about 10 minutes for this part. Don't explain anything on the screen. If asked, reply "What do you think it means?" Note what they type, where they hesitate, and their exact words.

1. **H3:** "Find the place where you usually leave your bike." Watch what they type. Don't help.
2. **H6:** "Set the app for the last time you parked there." Watch which buttons they choose and whether they hesitate.
3. **H5:** Open Großer Tiergarten with Evening, Weekday and Standard bike selected (it shows High risk, Confidence: Low). Ask: "In your own words, what is this card telling you?"
4. **H7:** "Based on the lock recommendation, what would you do or buy?"
5. **H4:** "These ratings are based on thefts reported to the police. What does that mean to you?"
6. **H1:** "Thinking of last week again, which of your trips would you have checked this for?"

### Emotional context

The synthetic answers were calm and analytical. Real riders bring feelings and situations the AI left out. Plan for these:

- **Theft is personal.** Let people tell their theft story in full before moving on. Listen for anger, guilt ("it was my fault") and loss, and note their exact words.
- **No blame.** Never suggest their lock or spot was wrong. If they blame themselves, don't confirm or correct it; ask "What happened next?"
- **Their own area.** When their home area or station shows High risk, ask: "How does it feel to see your area rated like this?" Listen for worry, anger, or a sense that the area is treated unfairly (team assumption A11).
- **The real moment of use.** Ask where they would be when checking: early morning at a station, on the way to a café, at home at night. If the demo is online, let them open it on their own phone.
- **Reporting is sensitive.** Some people don't report thefts because they distrust the police or think it's pointless. Ask why without judging, and don't push if they don't want to say.

## Data Strategy

How we know LockOut delivers value. The product promises no accounts and no tracking, so every metric counts visits, events, answers or reports and never identifies a person.

### Behavioural metrics

Usage is counted as anonymous totals only: no cookies, and no way to tell visitors apart. Returning visitors can't be counted by design, so whether people come back (H1) is measured through interview question H1 and prototype task 6 instead.

| Metric | What it tells us | How to measure | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly visits | Overall reach, and whether usage grows | Anonymous visit count | Set after the first month of data |
| Area cards opened per visit | Whether visitors get to an answer | Count "card opened" events | Set after the first month of data |
| Lock advice viewed | Whether people reach the part that changes behaviour | Count views of the lock recommendation | Set after the first month of data |
| Searches with no result | How often people look for a place the app can't find (H3) | Count searches that return nothing, without storing what was typed | Set after the first month of data |
| Filter changes | Whether riders adjust time, day or bike type (H6) | Count filter changes | Set after the first month of data |

### Satisfaction metrics

| Metric | What it tells us | How to measure | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wording understood | Whether riders understand what the tier means | Pilot: riders explain a card in their own words (prototype task 3) | 4 of 5 pilot riders explain it correctly (from scope definition) |
| Trust in the rating | Whether riders believe it enough to act on it | Pilot: "How much would you rely on this rating, from 1 to 5? Why?" | Set after the first pilot round (no baseline yet) |
| Would recommend | Word of mouth is the main channel (business model canvas) | Pilot and survey: "How likely are you to recommend this to a friend who cycles, from 0 to 10?" Report the raw answers; an NPS score needs far more people | Set after the first pilot round |
| Changed how they parked | Whether riders feel it made a difference | In-app one-question poll: "Did this change how you parked?" (yes / no / not sure), counting answers only (business model canvas) | Set after the first pilot round |
| "This looks wrong" reports | Where riders think the app is wrong | Count and group the reports and GitHub issues by theme | No target; review themes each week |

### Business metrics

LockOut is free and open source with no revenue (business model canvas), so revenue and customer lifetime value don't apply. These metrics cover what keeps a free civic tool running: cost, reach and the quality of its ratings.

| Metric | What it tells us | How to measure | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Running cost | Whether the project can keep running for free | Monthly hosting and service bills | A few euros per month after setup (business model canvas) |
| Reach through communities | Whether riders hear about it, since word of mouth is the main channel | Count the cycling groups, ADFC events and forums where the link is shared | Set after the first pilot round |
| Rating accuracy | Whether the ratings point to where thefts actually happen | Hold-out test on the most recent 3–6 months of police data | At least 70% of High-tier areas stay in the top ~25% by actual thefts, and rank correlation is at least 0.6 (scope definition) |
| Data freshness | Whether the ratings stay current | Weekly data refresh log (GitHub Actions) | Failures are visible, and the app keeps serving the last good data (technical brief) |

### Leading indicators

Early signals from the pilot sessions that predict long-term use. Each comes from a prototype task or hypothesis in the interview guide above.

| Indicator | What it predicts | How to measure | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time to a lock decision | Whether the app is quick enough to use before parking | Pilot: time from opening the app to saying what they'd do | Under 1 minute (scope definition) |
| Finds their usual spot without help | Whether first use succeeds | Prototype task 1 (H3): count riders who find their place unaided | Set after the first pilot round |
| Real moments they'd have checked | Whether people would come back, our biggest open risk | Prototype task 6 (H1): number of last week's trips each rider would have checked | Set after the first pilot round |
| Acts on the lock advice | Whether the app changes how people lock | Prototype task 4 (H7): count riders who name a concrete action or purchase | Set after the first pilot round |
| Asks for or shares the link | Whether word of mouth will work | Count riders who ask for the link or pass it on without being prompted | Set after the first pilot round |

No finding is rated High: all evidence so far comes from AI personas, not real riders.

### Team Reflection

**What surprised me most:** the bias went the other way than expected. The course warns that AI personas tend to be too positive, but mine were too critical and too polished: all three said they would stop after one try, and their answers read like a UX review. The most likely reason is my own setup message, which asked them to be honest about doubts and allowed "I wouldn't use it". So the prompt shaped the bias as much as the model did, and synthetic criticism needs the same caution as synthetic praise. Next time, I would run one persona a second time with a neutral setup to see how much the instruction changes the answers.
